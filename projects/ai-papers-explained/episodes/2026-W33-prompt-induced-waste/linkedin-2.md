## Final post (paste verbatim)

Three days after shipping Episode 1 of Papers in the Wild, I noticed something awkward.

I had spent ~9 hours producing the episode. Reading the paper. Designing the experiment. Running 36 trials. Writing the post. Designing the carousel. The work I am proud of.

I had spent 0 hours on distribution.

No outreach to anyone who might genuinely care about prompt-induced waste. No engagement with the LinkedIn posts where this would land. No SEO thinking on the blog. No email to the two researchers who wrote the paper.

This is the part of digital marketing that nobody warns builders about. The work is 40% of the job. Distribution is the other 60%. And distribution is the part that feels gross, so most builders skip it and then wonder why their best work lands softly.

So this week I am running an experiment on myself. Three changes:

→ **15 minutes of outreach before any production work.** Email, DM, comment on someone else's post. Distribution first, creation second.
→ **One direct email to each paper's authors when I cover them.** Not for links. For correction. They will catch my mistakes before readers do.
→ **Track distribution metrics, not just production metrics.** How many people I reached out to. Not just how many hours I spent writing.

The Papers in the Wild episode this week was on prompt-induced waste in coding agents. The irony that I was wasting my own work by skipping distribution is not lost on me.

If you ship creative work in public and you are also a builder who skips distribution: tell me what your trick is. I am taking notes.

## Why this hook works (for future reference)

- **Opens with an admission of failure** — most "I shipped a thing" posts are vanity. This one is honest about what didn't happen.
- **Specific time + hours** ("~9 hours producing... 0 hours on distribution") — credibility through specificity.
- **Universal pain point** — every builder struggles with distribution. Naming it is more useful than pretending.
- **Three concrete changes** — not a listicle, not platitudes. The 15-minute rule is something the reader can steal today.
- **Calls back to the episode theme** — prompt-induced waste → distribution-induced waste. The callback makes the post feel cohesive, not opportunistic.
- **Ends with a real question** — invites replies, not just likes.
